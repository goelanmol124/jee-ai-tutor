# Sources and public-discourse record

Compiled: 2026-07-18  
All access dates below are 2026-07-18 unless another retrieval date is stated.

## Source handling

- Full texts were obtained only from official publishers, institutional repositories, PMC/Europe PMC, or the report owner.
- No Sci-Hub or other unauthorised repository was used.
- Ten reviewed full texts are stored in `papers/`; eight are core sources and two are supplementary.
- Public quotes omit usernames and other unnecessary identifiers.
- Academic results, anecdotes, reviews, and commercial claims are labelled separately.
- Public posts and app reviews can be edited or removed. Quotes below are snapshots for research, not prevalence estimates.
- Reddit’s unauthenticated search endpoint began returning access/rate-limit pages during collection. Existing indexed pages, an earlier successful public JSON response, and public syndications were used; no access controls were bypassed.
- YouTube page text/transcripts were sampled where publicly indexed. Comment threads were inconsistently accessible, so no inaccessible comments were invented or attributed.

## Full-text evidence in `papers/`

### [A1] ASER Centre (2024). *Annual Status of Education Report (Rural) 2023: Beyond Basics*

- File: `papers/aser-2023-beyond-basics.pdf`
- Official full text: https://asercentre.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/ASER-2023-Report-1.pdf
- Publication date: 2024-01-17
- Design/sample: household survey of 34,745 youth aged 14–18 in 1,664 villages, 28 districts, and 26 states.
- Used for: smartphone availability, ownership, gendered access, digital task performance, computer scarcity, education-related phone use.
- Key findings used:
  - 89% had a smartphone in the household; 92% could use one.
  - Of youth able to use a smartphone, 31.1% owned one; 43.7% of males and 19.8% of females did.
  - 67.1% could bring a connected phone for the assessment; 72.9% of males and 62% of females.
  - Only 9% had a computer/laptop at home.
- Limits: rural district sample, not nationally representative of all JEE learners; does not isolate coaching or JEE candidates.

### [A2] Central Square Foundation (2026). *Bharat Survey for EdTech 2025*

- File: `papers/csf-2025-bharat-survey-edtech.pdf`
- Official full text: https://www.edtechbase.centralsquarefoundation.org/BaSE%20Report%202025.pdf
- Publication date: 2026-02
- Data collection: 2025-07 to 2026-01
- Design/sample: multi-stage sample of 12,500 households/children and 2,500 teachers across ten states, focused on government and affordable-private-school communities.
- Used for: shared-device reality, intermittent connectivity, EdTech barriers, GenAI usage and misconceptions, drivers of continued use, trust/risk.
- Key findings used:
  - 72% of children had smartphone access; 68% shared access; 4% dedicated access.
  - Of those with access, 29% had intermittent internet; 69% encountered internet issues.
  - 35% of EdTech-using children used GenAI for learning.
  - 72% of GenAI-aware children mistook it for internet search.
  - Continued GenAI use was driven by easy explanations (45%), quick responses (41%), and interactivity (40%).
  - 67% of EdTech users faced challenges, including content discovery, data cost, distraction, and usability.
- Limits: broad Grades 1–12 population, not JEE-specific; self-report; specialised EdTech use was a small share of overall digital learning.

### [A3] Lokniti-CSDS (2023). *Issues of Mental Health Amongst Students in Kota: Survey Findings*

- File: `papers/lokniti-2023-kota-mental-health.pdf`
- Official full text: https://www.lokniti.org/media/upload_files/Kota%20Study%202023-Issues%20of%20Mental%20Health%20amongst%20students%C2%A0in%C2%A0Kota-Survey%20Findings.pdf
- Project page: https://www.lokniti.org/otherstudies/issues-of-mental-health-amongst-students-in-kota-study-231
- Publication/data period: 2023
- Design/sample: random sampling in four to five Kota localities; 1,002 coaching students aged 15–25. The sample was 34.7% JEE and 58.9% NEET; 72% Class 12 and 18.5% Class 11.
- Used for: school/coaching overlap, weekly tests, stress, family pressure, coping, confidants, help-seeking, loneliness/homesickness, leisure.
- Key findings used:
  - 57.2% were also enrolled in school/intermediate college.
  - 54% found weekly tests very helpful, 30.1% preferred less frequent tests, and 10.7% said tests added stress.
  - 8.8% often and 24.9% sometimes experienced family pressure.
  - Under extreme stress, 49.4% talked to family/friends/siblings, 46% slept, 40.1% used entertainment, 36.1% walked/exercised/meditated, and 13% started studying.
  - 3.4% had visited a mental-health professional; 19% preferred to fight alone; affordability, stigma, and access were additional barriers.
- Limits: combines JEE and NEET; cross-sectional self-report; Kota may represent a more intensive coaching environment than home-based preparation.

### [A4] Shekhawat, B. S., et al. (2023). “Stress and coping strategy among coaching and non-coaching students in Kota: A comparative study”

- File: `papers/shekhawat-et-al-2023-kota-coaching-fulltext.xml`
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.4103/ipj.ipj_203_23
- PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10871398/
- Journal: *Industrial Psychiatry Journal*
- Design/sample: cross-sectional comparison of 90 coaching and 90 non-coaching students using the Scale of Academic Stress and Hindi Coping Strategies Scale.
- Key findings used:
  - Coaching: 44.45% high, 34.44% average, 21.11% low academic stress.
  - Non-coaching: 3.33% high, 26.67% average, 70% low academic stress.
  - Both groups showed low active coping and high avoidance coping.
  - Higher stress was associated with Class 12, female gender, and parent-selected stream, among other characteristics.
- Limits: small cross-sectional sample; coaching and non-coaching groups differed in some characteristics; cannot infer causality.

### [A5] Mayya, S. S., et al. (2022). “Academic stress and associated sociodemographic variables: A study of pre-university students in Karnataka, India”

- File: `papers/mayya-et-al-2022-academic-stress-fulltext.xml`
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.4103/jehp.jehp_87_22
- PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9514250/
- Journal: *Journal of Education and Health Promotion*
- Design/sample: stratified cluster cross-sectional survey of 1,853 analysed science-stream pre-university students in coastal Karnataka.
- Key findings used:
  - 28% of Grade 11 and 26% of Grade 12 students reported high or extreme stress.
  - Lack of revision time was a high/extreme stressor for 38.7%.
  - Relative/neighbour queries, parental expectations, and lack of extracurricular time were prominent.
  - Gender, residence, and medium of instruction through Grade 10 were associated with stress.
- Limits: science-stream students, not specifically JEE candidates; one region; data collected during 2021.

### [A6] Parikh, R., et al. (2019). “‘It is like a mind attack’: stress and coping among urban school-going adolescents in India”

- File: `papers/parikh-et-al-2019-mind-attack-fulltext.xml`
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-019-0306-z
- PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6540371/
- Journal: *BMC Psychology*
- Design/sample: 22 focus groups with 191 adolescents aged 11–17 from low- and middle-income communities in Delhi and Goa.
- Used for: stress vocabulary, concentration loss, emotional versus instrumental help-seeking, distraction, avoidance, active problem solving, parent/peer influence.
- Key findings used: home, school, peers, and neighbourhood were intertwined sources of stress; anger, rumination, and concentration loss were common; emotion-focused coping was more common than problem-focused coping.
- Limits: not JEE-specific; qualitative findings establish patterns and language, not prevalence.

### [A7] Krispenz, A., Gort, C., Schültke, L., & Dickhäuser, O. (2019). “How to Reduce Test Anxiety and Academic Procrastination Through Inquiry of Cognitive Appraisals”

- File: `papers/krispenz-et-al-2019-test-anxiety-fulltext.xml`
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01917
- PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710437/
- Journal: *Frontiers in Psychology*
- Design/sample: longitudinal quasi-randomised pilot; 40 intervention and 31 wait-list university students.
- Key finding used: a short inquiry intervention reduced test anxiety and subsequent procrastination; increased academic self-efficacy partly mediated anxiety reduction.
- Limits: small non-Indian university sample; preliminary transfer evidence, not a direct basis for a JEE intervention.

### [A8] Alshareef, N., Giga, S., & Fletcher, I. (2025). “Test anxiety, emotional regulation and academic performance among medical students: a qualitative study”

- File: `papers/alshareef-et-al-2025-test-anxiety-fulltext.xml`
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1080/10872981.2025.2505177
- PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12086906/
- Journal: *Medical Education Online*
- Design/sample: semi-structured interviews with 22 students at one Saudi medical school.
- Used for: blanking under pressure, pre/during/post-test anxiety, physical symptoms, adaptive and maladaptive regulation.
- Limits: adult, medical, and non-Indian sample; transfer evidence only.

### [A11] Deb, S., Strodl, E., & Sun, H. (2015). “Academic Stress, Parental Pressure, Anxiety and Mental Health among Indian High School Students”

- File: `papers/deb-et-al-2015-academic-stress-parental-pressure.pdf`
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.5923/j.ijpbs.20150501.04
- Institutional repository: https://eprints.qut.edu.au/86092/
- Journal: *International Journal of Psychology and Behavioral Sciences*
- Design/sample: questionnaire and General Health Questionnaire study of 190 Grade 11–12 students from government-aided and private schools in Kolkata.
- Key findings: 63.5% reported academic-pressure stress, 66% perceived parental pressure for better performance, 81.6% reported examination-related anxiety, and 32.6% crossed the study’s psychiatric-caseness threshold.
- Limits: small, older, city-specific cross-sectional study; not JEE-exclusive; self-report and screening measures do not establish diagnoses.

### [A12] Sarkar, D. (2024). “The Impact of Stress and Resilience on Students Appearing for Competitive Exams for Their Under Graduation in India”

- File: `papers/sarkar-2024-stress-resilience-competitive-exams.pdf`
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.25215/1204.122
- Official full text: https://ijip.in/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/18.01.122.20241204.pdf
- Journal: *International Journal of Indian Psychology*
- Used for: supplementary evidence that resilience, emotional regulation, and time-management capacity may buffer competitive-exam stress.
- Limits: methodological reporting and generalisability are weaker than the core sources; used only as corroboration, not for product thresholds or prevalence.

## Source gaps

- No strong comparative study was found that directly estimates JEE learner preferences for English versus Hinglish.
- No robust study compared photo-first, text-first, and rough-work-first doubt entry for JEE.
- Direct academic evidence on droppers as a separate cohort is limited.
- Public app reviews rarely provide reliable demographic or cohort information.
- YouTube comment access was inconsistent; videos/transcripts were sampled more reliably than comments.
- Quora pages exposed question/answer text but often lacked stable dates and authorship context.
- There is insufficient evidence to set one ideal tutor-session duration or notification cadence.

These gaps are why `synthesis.md` reserves material product tradeoffs for the product owner.
